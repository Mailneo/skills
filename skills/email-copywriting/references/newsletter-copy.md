# Newsletter and Broadcast Copy

Writing for people who opted in. The economics are inverted from cold
outreach: the reader already said yes once, and the job is to keep earning
that yes so the list compounds instead of decaying. The enemy is not the
spam folder first — it is quiet disengagement, the slow slide from "opens
every issue" to "deletes unread," which mailbox providers measure and
which eventually *becomes* a spam-folder problem (see the
email-deliverability skill).

Every send is a withdrawal from or a deposit into the same trust account.
Write each issue so that a subscriber who gives you 30 seconds still gets
value, and a subscriber who gives you five minutes gets more.

## Broadcast structures

Three skeletons cover almost every send. Pick one per email; do not mix.

### The single-story issue

One idea, told well. Structure: hook → development → payoff → CTA.

- **Hook (first 1–2 lines):** the most interesting true sentence you
  have. Open mid-story or with the surprising fact, never with
  throat-clearing ("Welcome back to another issue of..."). The first line
  is also probably your preview text — write it like it will be read in
  the inbox list, because it will.
- **Development:** the idea, the evidence, the story. Short paragraphs,
  one thought each.
- **Payoff:** the takeaway the reader can use today, stated plainly.
- **CTA:** one action, clearly tied to what they just read.

### The curated digest

A lead item plus shorts. Structure: lead (2–3 paragraphs, your best
material) → 3–6 short items, each with a bolded headline, one-sentence
"why this matters," and a link. Ruthless consistency of section order
makes the issue scannable on sight. Cap the item count; a digest that
tries to include everything teaches readers to skim everything.

### The announcement

One thing changed; say it in the first line. Structure: what's new → why
the reader should care (benefit, not feature) → what to do → one CTA.
Resist padding announcements into essays.

## Editorial voice

Consistency is the asset. A newsletter is a recurring appointment, and
readers show up for a voice as much as for information.

- **Pick a register and hold it.** Decide once: how casual, how technical,
  first person singular or plural, whether jokes belong. Sudden register
  shifts read as a different author — or as marketing taking over the pen.
- **Send from a person.** A named human sender ("Dana at Harborlight")
  outperforms a brand or role address; generic addresses get fewer opens
  and replies. The reply-to must reach someone who actually replies.
- **Keep structural rhythm.** Same sections, same order, same naming issue
  to issue. Readers build a mental map; honor it.
- **Subject-line style is part of the voice.** If your subjects are plain
  and lowercase for a year, one SHOUTING promo subject breaks the
  contract.

## Scannability

Most subscribers skim. Design for the skimmer; reward the reader.

- One idea per paragraph; paragraphs of 1–3 sentences.
- A bolded phrase or subhead every few paragraphs so a scrolling reader
  can reconstruct the issue from the bold text alone. If the bolds don't
  summarize the issue, restructure.
- Front-load each section: conclusion first, reasoning after.
- One visual break (subhead, rule, image) per screen of mobile scrolling.
- Cut every sentence that serves the writer instead of the reader —
  wind-ups, apologies for the gap since the last issue, meta-commentary
  about the newsletter itself.

See [formatting.md](formatting.md) for reading level, mobile widths, and
accessibility rules that apply to all of the above.

## Content-to-promotion ratio

Mostly value, occasionally an ask. Useful defaults: roughly 80/20 to 90/10
editorial-to-promotional for most audiences; B2B lists with long sales
cycles sit at the conservative end, high-frequency retail can run richer
promotion because subscribers signed up expecting offers. The ratio
applies across issues as well as within them — an issue that is pure
pitch is sometimes fine *because* the surrounding issues were pure value.

Two rules keep the ratio honest:

- When you do promote, promote plainly. A pitch dressed as editorial
  ("just sharing something cool" about your own paid product) is the
  newsletter version of a pretext — readers notice, and it spends more
  trust than an honest ask would.
- Real scarcity only. "Doors close Friday" must be true; a deadline that
  quietly extends teaches readers that your deadlines are theater and
  your claims negotiable.

## Welcome email copy

The welcome is the highest-leverage email you will ever send: open rates
run several times higher than a normal campaign, and most subscribers
expect it immediately. Its copy jobs, in order:

1. **Deliver the promised thing first.** If they signed up for a guide or
   discount, that link is the first line, not the fourth paragraph.
2. **Set expectations.** What they'll get, how often, from what sender
   name — one or two sentences, so the next issue is recognized, not
   reported.
3. **Establish the voice.** Write it like issue one, not like a receipt.
4. **One CTA.** The strongest choice is often a reply prompt ("Hit reply
   and tell me what you're working on") — replies build the engagement
   record providers score, and the answers are free audience research.

Invented example, complete:

> **Subject:** Your teardown checklist (and what's next)
>
> Here's the checklist you signed up for: [link].
>
> I'm Dana — every other Tuesday I send one teardown of a real signup
> flow, what it gets right, and one thing I'd change. No filler issues;
> if I have nothing worth your time, I skip the week.
>
> So I send you relevant examples: what kind of product do you work on?
> Hit reply — I read every answer.

## Win-back copy

For subscribers sliding toward silence. The sequencing and the decision of
when someone counts as disengaged belong to the email-lifecycle skill;
suppressing chronic non-openers is list hygiene (email-deliverability).
The copy rules:

- **No guilt.** "We miss you" and "Was it something we said?" center the
  sender. Center the reader: lead with the best of what they've been
  missing, concretely — your three most-read pieces, the feature that
  shipped, the result a customer got.
- **Acknowledge reality briefly.** One honest line ("You haven't opened
  these in a while — fair enough") earns more than pretending nothing
  happened.
- **One incentive at most,** held for late in a short series, not spent in
  the first touch.
- **End with the honest question.** The final win-back email asks plainly:
  "Want to keep getting these? One click keeps you on; no click and I'll
  stop emailing." Making it effortless to leave is both a compliance
  posture and the only way the engagement stats of your remaining list
  mean anything.

Invented example of a final touch:

> **Subject:** Should we stop emailing you?
>
> You haven't opened the last dozen issues — no hard feelings. If you
> want to keep getting teardowns, click here and you're in. If not, do
> nothing and I'll take you off the list next week. Either way, thanks
> for reading as long as you did.

## CTA design for owned audiences

- **One primary CTA per send.** Secondary links can exist in digest items,
  but one action is *the* action, visually and verbally.
- **Describe the outcome, not the mechanics.** "Read the full teardown,"
  "Get the checklist," "Save your seat" — never "Click here" or "Learn
  more," which say nothing and fail accessibility besides (see
  [formatting.md](formatting.md)).
- **Match the CTA to the content temperature.** An educational issue earns
  a soft CTA (read, reply); a launch issue earns a direct one (buy,
  book). A hard sell stapled to a soft issue reads as bait-and-switch.
- **Reply-as-CTA is underrated** for small and mid-size lists: it deepens
  the relationship and generates engagement signals no button can.
- **Repeat the primary CTA once** in a long issue — mid-body and end — and
  in the P.S. if you use one. Same destination, varied phrasing.
