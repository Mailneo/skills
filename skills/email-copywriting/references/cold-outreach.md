# Cold Outreach Copy

Unsolicited email to someone who never asked to hear from you. The copy
techniques below only matter after two gates are passed: the message is
lawful, and it is genuinely relevant to the recipient. Neither is optional.

## Compliance first

Read this before the anatomy, because it constrains everything after it.

- **Know your jurisdiction.** Unsolicited commercial email to individuals
  is unlawful or heavily restricted in much of the world (the EU, UK,
  Canada, and Australia among others, generally requiring consent or a
  narrow lawful basis). Even where B2B cold email is permitted, as in the
  US, it carries obligations: truthful headers and sender identity, a
  non-deceptive subject, identification as a commercial message, a valid
  physical postal address, and a working opt-out honored promptly. The
  email-compliance skill covers the rules per jurisdiction; consult it
  before any cold campaign.
- **The opt-out is sacred.** Include a clear way out and honor it fast.
  Replacing the mechanism with a casual "reply 'no thanks' if this isn't
  for you" — a tactic taught specifically because unsubscribe links look
  commercial to filters — fails the legal standard and is evasion by
  design. An opt-out is permanent: never re-add someone who declined.
- **Relevance is the entry ticket.** Before writing a word, you must be
  able to complete this sentence honestly: "I am writing to this specific
  person because ___, and they plausibly benefit because ___." If you
  cannot, the problem is your list, and no copy below will fix it.
- **Identify yourself.** Real name, real company, real reason. Any tactic
  whose effect depends on the recipient believing something false about
  you, your message, or your purpose is off the table — legally risky and
  strategically self-defeating, because replies obtained under false
  pretenses convert to nothing.

## The anatomy

Three parts, 50–125 words total, in this order:

1. **Personalized observation.** One or two sentences proving a human
   looked at this recipient: something they shipped, wrote, changed, or
   visibly struggle with. This line does the work the subject cannot — it
   converts an open into a read.
2. **Value proposition with proof.** What you do, for whom, with one
   concrete proof point carrying a number. Speak the recipient's own KPIs:
   conversion and order value for a retailer, churn and expansion for a
   subscription business. The more audience-specific attributes your value
   line touches (role, sub-niche, geography, business model), the more it
   reads as "this is about me."
3. **Single low-friction ask.** One question the recipient can answer in
   seconds. Nothing else. No second CTA, no attachment, no link until
   they ask for one.

A complete invented example:

> **Subject:** Fernwood's wholesale lead time
>
> Hi Marisol — your wholesale page quotes a 3-week lead time, but your
> retail shop ships in two days. When those diverge, wholesale buyers
> usually stall at the quote stage.
>
> We build quote-to-order flows for specialty food producers; Kestrel
> Provisions cut quote turnaround from 9 days to 1 and closed 30% more
> wholesale accounts last quarter.
>
> Is wholesale lead time actually a problem for you? A one-line reply
> either way is genuinely useful.

Note what is absent: no "I hope this finds you well," no company origin
story, no feature list, no calendar link, no attachment.

## Personalization tiers

Trade research time against depth, and never fake the tier you are in.

1. **Bespoke first lines.** A researched observation unique to this person
   — a recent launch, a talk, a hiring pattern, a visible gap. Budget 30–60
   seconds of research per prospect; this line roughly doubles reply rates
   and is where manual effort belongs. The rest of the email is written
   once per segment.
2. **Segment-level lines.** Openers keyed to a shared attribute — city,
   industry event, seasonal pressure, platform where you found them — that
   read as personal but reuse across a whole segment. Honest when the
   attribute is true; dishonest when phrased to imply individual research.
3. **Merge fields.** First name, company, role as the baseline everywhere,
   including the subject. Necessary, never sufficient.

The integrity rule across all tiers: personalization may compress your
effort, but it may not overstate it. "I noticed" must mean you noticed.

## Ask design

Order asks by friction, lowest first:

1. Permission question — "Worth sending the 2-page breakdown?"
2. Binary interest check — "Is this on your radar this quarter, yes/no?"
3. One-word-answerable question — "Who owns onboarding at Harborlight?"
4. Offer of specific short times — "Would 15 minutes Tuesday or Wednesday
   morning work?"
5. Calendar-booking link — the highest-friction first ask there is.
   Choosing a slot is real work; sent cold, it reliably produces the
   fewest responses. Earn it with a smaller yes first.

**The yes/no/forward test:** before sending, check that your ask can be
fully answered with "yes," "no," or "let me forward this to the right
person." If answering requires the recipient to think, schedule, or
compose, shrink the ask. Identify the easiest one-word reply that still
moves things forward, and design the question to invite exactly that.

## Follow-up sequences

Follow-ups add a large share of total replies — the first email earns
most, but a well-built sequence captures a meaningful minority the first
touch missed. The gains concentrate early: two or three follow-ups after
the original capture nearly all of the value, while long grinding
sequences multiply unsubscribes and spam complaints faster than they add
replies (and those complaints damage every future send — see the
email-deliverability skill).

A sound shape — four touches over about two weeks:

- **Day 0 — the original.** As above.
- **Day 3 — the bump.** Two sentences, on-thread, adding one small new
  element: a sharper question, a clarifying detail. "Just bumping this"
  with nothing new is acceptable exactly once, if at all.
- **Day 8 — the value touch.** A genuinely new angle: a relevant
  resource, a short case detail, an observation you did not use the first
  time. Escalate value, never pressure.
- **Day 16 — the close.** An honest breakup: "I'll assume the timing's
  wrong and close this out — if that changes, you know where I am."
  Then actually stop.

Rules that hold across every touch:

- **Each touch must add something.** New information, new angle, or a
  graceful exit. Repetition without addition is nagging.
- **Include an easy out.** One line per email that makes "no" costless:
  "If this isn't relevant, tell me and I'll stop." A recipient who can
  decline cheaply is far less likely to reach for the spam button.
- **No guilt, no fake escalation.** "I guess this isn't a priority for
  you" and manufactured deadlines ("closing your file Friday") are
  pressure tactics that trade long-term trust for short-term replies.
- **Stay on-thread** so context travels with each touch.
- **Send during the recipient's working hours,** in their timezone.

After an explicit "no": thank them, optionally ask once what would have
made it a yes (real product intelligence), record the opt-out, and never
contact them again.

## Reply handling

The copywriting is wasted if replies rot in the inbox.

- **Speed wins.** Answer positive replies within minutes when possible;
  same day is the floor. Interest decays by the hour.
- **Fixed processing slots.** Two short daily blocks for triaging replies
  beat sporadic checking.
- **Mirror their next step.** If they propose a time, confirm that time
  and send the invite yourself. If they leave a number, call it. Do not
  answer a warm, specific reply with a generic booking link.
- **Discovery before demo.** First call short (~15 minutes) to qualify;
  book the longer session from there.
- **Keep pricing out of email.** Offer a range only if pressed; specifics
  belong in the conversation.
- **Nudge unbooked positives** once within 24–48 hours.
- **Build an answer bank.** Prospect questions repeat; reusable answers
  keep response time in minutes.

## Don'ts

Each of these is taught somewhere as a growth tactic. Each is deception,
and each is excluded:

- **Hidden or replaced opt-outs** — covered above; a legal requirement,
  not a style choice.
- **The broken-link ploy** — sending a deliberately dead link to a
  supposed personalized video so recipients reply to report it. Engineered
  deception; every reply it generates was obtained under false pretenses.
- **Fake mobile signatures** — appending "sent from my phone" to bulk
  desktop mail to fake off-the-cuff effort. A small lie that poisons the
  relationship it was meant to start.
- **Pretext angles** — survey, podcast, or referral framings used as
  camouflage for a pitch. Fine when genuine; deception when not, even if
  each sentence is technically true.
- **Manufactured scarcity** — invented "2 spots left" claims. Deceptive
  under consumer-protection law; urgency is only a copy asset when the
  constraint is real.
- **Copy randomization for filter evasion** — rotating signatures and
  phrasing so providers cannot fingerprint the campaign. Defeating abuse
  detection is spam-operation behavior; vary copy for readers and tests
  only.
- **Volume games** — lookalike throwaway domains and burned-inbox rotation
  belong to the same family: they concede recipients do not want the mail.
  See the email-deliverability skill for legitimate sending practice.
